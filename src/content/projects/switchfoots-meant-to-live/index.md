I’m sure many of you can picture it, being a teenager in 2003. Puberty hadn’t quite hit me yet. I was short, skinny, with a mop of blonde hair and introverted to the gills. And thanks to my Evangelical upbringing, I was also sheltered. Sheltered from the boy bands, and Britney, and certainly sheltered from *rap*. Our suburban house was filled with the safe sounds of CCM: Christian Contemporary Music. AKA the sounds of Christian radio. When my parents put on music, the stereo played the upbeat and hopeful sounds of Steven Curtis Chapman, Amy Grant, MercyMe, Newsboys, FFH, Chris Tomlin, Nicole Nordman, and Point of Grace. Sorry if you’re already familiar with the lineup from the WOW CD of 2003 (CCM’s ripoff of NOW That’s What I Call Music.) If we were lucky, my dad would play a little DC Talk, which was the closest our 10-foot pole got to hip-hop.

All of these musical selections were *positive* and *encouraging* and *hopeful*. A cocktail, excuse me, mocktail of belief that said if you had enough positive faith, you would be okay. But none of that resonated with the feelings simmering inside me. Feelings of *doubt* and *loneliness* and *anxiety*. When I stood in the front rows of church with the rest of the youth group or when my parents put on music to work on our chores on a Saturday morning, I didn’t recognize the sentiments coming out of those wood panelled speakers.

Then, in the late winter of 2003 everything changed. I couldn’t tell you where I first heard it; the ubiquitous nature of the song obscures my memory. I could’ve heard it from a burned CD another kid handed me, making me promise to pass it along to the next person. I could’ve heard it at a listening station at the Christian bookstore, big headphones enveloping my ears, surrounded by rows of leather Bibles and flowery devotionals. I certainly didn’t hear it on the safe (*positive and encouraging!*) K-Love radio station.

But gun to my head, if I had to take a guess, I most likely heard Switchfoot’s *Meant to Live* for the first time on the speakers at my church youth group. The opening riffs were so different from anything else I was allowed to listen to at the time. The back and forth between the prodigal-laced opening lines and the heavy riffs felt impossible in a room that preached so much positivity.

> Fumbling his confidence and wondering why the world has passed him by
> Hoping that he’s bent for more than arguments and failed attempts to fly

What the hell? (sorry God). What was this? Who was singing this? And who was playing this CD over the church speakers, because the youth pastor was going to speak to them privately later about sound judgement.
I hear the chorus hit and it’s more questions and doubt. Switchfoot’s lead singer, Jon Foreman, is pleading with the universe.

> We were meant to live for so much more 
> Have we lost ourselves? 
> Somewhere we live inside 
> Somewhere we live insiiiiiiiiiiiiiiiiide 

That last bit is important. You couldn’t sing Christian MusicTM in 2003 and let your voice break into a yell or a growl or anything resembling the two. Certainly not in the household I grew up in. That desperation in Foreman’s voice drew me in. My thirteen-year-old thoughts weren’t nearly as eloquently put together as his song lyrics, but Foreman captured the questions and the doubts that I experienced each week squeezed into a folding chair listening to speaker after speaker push more belief onto us. 

Twenty-three years later, the song continues to be an anthem for millennials of all ages, an antidote to the Evangelical industrial complex that continues to push *positive* and *encouraging* (and *safe!*). The album eventually went triple platinum, no thanks to limewire and the burnt CDs passed around every youth group in the country. 

I still walk around with those same doubts that Jon Foreman sang into that early millennium void. *Have I lost myself?* But I know now that those doubts aren’t because of a lack of faith or lack of prayer. I don’t have to have it all figured out, and I’m not on the outs with God for having some questions about this life we’re living.

> We want more than this world’s got to offer 
> We want more than this world’s got to offer 
> We want more than the wars of our fathers 
> And everything inside 
> Screams for second life 

Second life didn’t come for thirteen-year-old me. Not in 2003 or anytime soon thereafter. But when everything inside me screamed for a second life outside evangelicalism, I had my guiding words of wisdom. The song that had opened my eyes and ears to properly expressing my doubt continued to riff inside me, crunchy guitars wailing their call and response: *We were meant to live for so much more.*
